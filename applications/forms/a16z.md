## Sathya

I captained FTC 8719 "Quantum Leap" — an independent robotics team with no school affiliation and
no district budget, families covering about $10,000 a year out of pocket.

Technically, the team ran what we believed was the first four-wheel dead-wheel odometry localizer
in FIRST: four encoders fused through a Kalman filter, holding under 0.2 inches of drift over
thirty seconds of driving across ten of ten trials, against three to eight inches for the standard
three-wheel setup. Four wheels wasn't the default because it makes the whole system less
forgiving — wheel geometry, vector math, calibration, mounting rigidity and code all have to be
right at once, and the prevailing intuition was that if one tracking wheel can introduce drift,
more sensors just give you more bad data. The insight was that with a rigid base and careful
calibration the extra measurements stop being noise and become redundancy. It wasn't universally
better — it cost space, design time and iteration — but it was better for the robot and the game
we were building for.

In the season I captained I was also the team's primary programmer, and my focus was making the
autonomous system dependable: hardening the localizer, starting to build vision into the robot,
improving PID tuning, and pushing for a more customised mechanical base, because the software was
only ever as good as the hardware it was controlling. That field had a large central structure that
was difficult to navigate around, so precise autonomous movement was the binding constraint. I
wasn't just writing code for the robot — I was working through the relationship between design,
localisation, controls and match strategy until the system was dependable enough to actually use.

We were #1-ranked at every qualifier we entered, won the Inspire Award — FIRST's top honour — in
Virginia and Ohio, and placed 19th in the world at Detroit.

The part I'd point at, though, is the part that made any of it possible. At sixteen I started
cold-calling companies for parts. I carried the largest prospecting list on the team — twenty

# Q6 — What are you building and why are you the one to build it?

## What we're building

Hiring looks like a speed problem. It's a data problem.

Nobody has an accurate, current view of who is actually in the market, so matching fails and every
player compensates with volume — more outreach, more applications, more noise. The noise is the
symptom.

LinkedIn is a profile graph. It records where you've been, and that data is a commodity: we bought
200,000 profiles from a data broker on a credit card. What nobody has is an **intent graph** — who
would move, on what terms, and when. Intent is *private* (say it on LinkedIn and your boss, your
reports and three recruiters you didn't want to hear from all find out), *conditional* ("I'd move
for a founding role above $X, not into an enterprise sales org"), and *perishable* (someone open in
March isn't open in June). None of those are profile fields. "Open to work" is a boolean — the
crudest possible encoding of the only thing that matters.

The consequence is that the people worth $30,000 a placement stay silent, and the entire industry
fights over the small, visible, mostly junior slice willing to be seen looking.

We're building the intent graph. Every surface we run is a sensor: outbound, inbound, a voice
agent, a messaging agent, and the applications we submit on a candidate's behalf — which are the
purest revealed intent there is, and which we originate. We capture who is in the market and on
what terms, hold it as a consented, timestamped record, and match against it. Nothing is
published. A standing brief sits dormant and you're contacted only when something clears your bar —
which is the point, not a flaw, and it's why retention isn't coupled to job-seeking the way it is
for every consumer job product that has died.

We also run the searches ourselves. That isn't a compromise: a two-sided market needs one side warm
on day one, so the firm is how we have live roles to match the first briefs against, and it
generates the outcome data that makes our matching better than anyone else's.

**What runs today, so you can separate it from the thesis:** our own sourcing engine over the
200K-profile index (hybrid Postgres full-text plus pgvector retrieval fused with reciprocal rank
fusion, then an LLM rerank), with an offline eval harness reporting nDCG@10, MRR, P@k and R@10
against ground truth expressed as rules over candidate attributes rather than a frozen label list;
a live voice agent and messaging agent unified with email on one CRM; **a browser agent that fills
and submits applications end to end, with submissions landing against Greenhouse, Ashby and
Workday**; a recruiter SPA and a candidate portal; per-application inbound aliases with every
employer reply classified and timestamped. ~161K lines, 185 test files, CI, architectural
boundaries enforced by import-linter, and a written compliance posture covering AI disclosure,
recording consent, TCPA, and no protected-category inference anywhere in the stack.

**One architectural decision worth a sentence, because it's the one we'd defend hardest.** The
obvious way to submit applications is hand-built ATS adapters. We talked to a company doing exactly
that — 29 adapters, plus hand-rolled HTTPS to avoid captcha and spam detection — and concluded it
works until it doesn't. So we took the slower, general path: browser agents. It cost us a week on
Workday's captcha alone and we had to move to residential proxies to get through. Generality beats
coverage over any horizon that matters.

**What is roadmap:** the intent object as a formal schema with confidence and decay scoring, the
aggregate market-truth product, an agent-facing read API, and one-click "Apply with Foray" on
employers' own careers pages. We'd rather tell you which half is which than have you find out.

# Q8 — Anything else we should know?

**Four things, and the last two are the ones you'd find anyway.**

**One — what we know that the category doesn't.** Every product in AI recruiting is sold against
sourcing, because sourcing demos well. But sourcing is the commoditised half: an index is worth the
same to the first buyer and the thousandth, and a Juicebox engineer described their own system to us
as closer to a search index. What the ~25 recruiters we spoke to actually told us is that they are
all fishing the same pond with the same tools, so the industry competes on who emails first.
Proprietary supply is the one thing none of them has. That's what we're building.

**Two — the position we're accumulating, which exactly one seat on the board can occupy.** Every
application we send carries its own inbound alias, and every employer reply is classified —
acknowledged, rejected, interview, assessment — with a timestamp, joined to candidate and req. An
ATS sees one company. A job board hands off at the click. A sourcing tool stops at the intro. Being
the origin of the application *and* holding the return channel is the only position from which
hiring outcomes can be measured across companies. That data compounds with every application we
send and is worth nothing to whoever starts tomorrow. Eventually it's what lets us underwrite a
longer guarantee at a lower fee than any agency can match — every agency quotes ~20% with a 90-day
guarantee, not because it's right but because none of them can price risk.

**Three — the two facts we'd rather you hear from us.** **No cash has been collected.** We have two
client engagements at 20% of base and ~$232K of contingent fees against four roles, but every
dollar of that is contingent on a hire, the terms are agreed rather than papered, and we're two
days into it. Before this week everything we delivered was free, on purpose — charging early would
have gated every req behind a contract and capped the candidate volume we could move, and the graph
is the asset. And neither of us is full-time yet — we're at WestBridge through the end of 2026 and
both full-time on Foray from 1 January. That's decided, not aspirational; if a cohort starts before
then, acceptance moves the date forward. We're not asking to do this part-time.

**Four — the honest bear case.** Cold start on the supply side is the hard part, and we're in it: a
standing brief is worth little at a thousand and enormous at a hundred thousand. Our best answer is
the rejection moment — the highest-intent moment in a job seeker's life, where nobody is standing —
and we've already shipped the Gmail connector it runs on. If that doesn't work we'll know quickly,
and we'd rather tell you what would kill this than have you guess.
