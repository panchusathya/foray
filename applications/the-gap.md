## The reviewer asks you a different question than they ask the dropout

This is the whole finding, so it goes first.

When a 20-year-old who left Stanford to build robots hits the pile, the reviewer's question is
**"is this person exceptional?"** It's unknown, so they read for slope: what did they build, how
fast, how young, what did they walk away from.

Your resumes answer that question on the first line and keep answering it for two pages. Two
top-ranked people at an $8B ex-Sequoia-India fund. A Berkeley CS + Haas honours double. An
origination record with seven acquisitions and fourteen follow-on rounds from the top of the
market. **A reviewer does not finish your first paragraph wondering whether you're capable.**

So they skip to the second question — **the one the dropout never has to answer, because dropping
out already answered it:**

> **"Are you actually going to do this?"**

And the honest answer your pack currently gives is *"at the end of the year, probably."*

**That's the gap. Not impressiveness. Commitment legibility.** The single most valuable asset you
have — the WestBridge seats — is also the only serious argument against you, and every document in
`applications/` currently treats it as neutral background information.

Worse, the archetype is known. *Two VC associates building a startup on the side* is a pattern
every partner at all five programs has seen, funded once, and learned from. You are not being read
as two exceptional operators; you are being read as **that pattern**, until something in the
application breaks the frame.

### It's not only a perception problem — it's mechanically disqualifying at two of the five

Check the current terms yourself, but from what these programs are:

| Program | Format | Compatible with a day job? |
|---|---|---|
| **HF0** | Live-in residency | **No.** You move in. There is no part-time version. |
| **a16z speedrun** | 12 weeks in SF, $1M | **No, in practice.** |
| **Greylock Edge** | Pre-seed/seed program | Marginal |
| **Soma Fellows** | Fellowship | Yes |
| **Z-Fellows** | One week | Yes |

**HF0 and speedrun are the two you most want and the two where "full-time at year end" is a
mechanical reject**, regardless of how good the writing is. An application that doesn't resolve
this in its own text gets resolved for it.

## What the three archetypes have that you don't

**The dropout** has a *costly signal.* Commitment is free for them to prove — they already paid.
Nothing in your pack costs you anything yet.

**The quant/olympiad kid** has *three-second legibility.* "IMO silver," "Jane Street," "2400 CF."
One line, verifiable, no argument required. You have paragraphs.

**The ten-year obsessive** reads as *inevitable.* They've done one thing since they were twelve;
the company is the obvious next step.

Notice that all three are answers to *"is this person exceptional?"* — the question you already
pass. **You are trying to win a category you're not competing in, and it's making the material
worse:** more accomplishments, more logos, more lines of code. None of it moves a reviewer who
stopped doubting your ability on line one.

## What you have that none of them have — and are barely using

**A scored judgment record.**

Every other profile in that pile has *unscored* talent. The dropout has potential. The quant kid
has demonstrated ability on problems **somebody else set**. The obsessive has depth in one lane.

Tushar picked 21 markets, with his own name attached, inside a year, and got graded by the market:
**seven acquired, fourteen raised from a16z, Sequoia, Khosla, Bessemer, Coatue and Madrona. a16z
funded three of them after he found them.** Sathya formed a fund's cloud-infrastructure thesis and
self-sourced two of three diligences in a summer.

**Nobody in that applicant pool has been publicly graded on their own picks.** It is structurally
unavailable to a 20-year-old, at any level of talent, because it takes a year of decisions and a
year of outcomes.

So the pitch is not *"we're impressive too."* It's:

> **"You are being asked to bet on our judgment. Here is our judgment, scored, over a year, with
> the receipts. We evaluate roughly a thousand companies a year between us. We picked this one."**

That is a class of evidence the dropout cannot manufacture. It is also the answer to *"recruiting
is boring"* — you are the only applicants who can credibly say **"we know what a consensus market
looks like from the inside, and we deliberately didn't pick one."**

One consequence worth naming: the robotics and defense applications are bets on theses the
reviewer **already holds**. Yours is a bet on one they **don't**. That means you have a writing
job they don't have — **the reviewer must finish A1 believing something they didn't believe
before.** The rebuild of A1 around *"every recruiter fishes the same pond, and none of them has
proprietary supply"* is exactly that paragraph. It's the most important paragraph in the pack.

# Still missing, ranked by how much it moves the outcome

## Tier 1 — the three that decide this. None is a writing problem.

**1. 🔴 A date, or one founder out now.**

Options, best to worst:

- **One of you resigns before submitting.** Halves the objection and makes the other's year-end
  date credible instead of aspirational. This is the single highest-leverage action available to
  you and it is not a document.
- **A stated date with a stated trigger:** *"Sathya's last day at WestBridge is 15 December. If we
  are accepted, both of us are full-time from day one of the program — that is a condition we've
  already agreed to, not a decision we'd have to make."* Put it **in the application, in writing,
  unprompted.** Volunteering the hard thing is the only move that converts it.
- **Say nothing and hope.** This is what the SPC application did.

There is no version where a reviewer at HF0 reads "full-time at year end" and doesn't act on it.
**Answer it before they ask it.**

**2. 🔴 One dollar.**

Eight companies hold free work. Vela has **$10K in writing.** You are claiming commercial traction
and reporting $0, which is a contradiction *inside your own story* — and unlike the dropout, you
don't get the benefit of "pre-revenue is normal at this stage," because you're the ones who
brought up eight customers. One invoice deletes the weakest row in the pack. Chase Vela.

**3. 🔴 A number for the intent thesis, and it's the same action as the consumer number.**

Your positioning is *we capture intent, LinkedIn is a profile graph.* Evidence: none in any form
yet. `intent_report.py` is written and has never been run.

And there's a second, larger hole hiding behind it. **The positioning claims proprietary consumer
distribution. Zero candidates have come to you.** Every one of the ~20 was pushed out by your
outbound on behalf of an employer. For a "LinkedIn 2.0, we own the discovery layer" thesis, the
first question a believing reviewer asks is *"how many candidates have signed up?"* — and the
answer is nothing.

**One action fixes both.** Get 50–100 candidates through an intake conversation with the agents
you have already built, and log what they say. That produces, in one week:

- *"N people have told us, in their own words, what would actually move them — timing, comp floor,
  competing offers, where they will and won't go. None of it is published anywhere and none of it
  can be scraped."* ← the sentence the entire positioning needs
- The consumer-side number
- The anonymised anecdote (`standing-out.md` #9), which is still the most valuable single sentence
  available to you
- The first real rows of the graph

**Run the report first** — the Zoho corpus may already hold more than you think. Then go get the
rest.

## Tier 2 — the standout moves. Nobody in that pile can copy these.

**4. 🟠 Publish something. You have zero public surface, and this is the highest-ceiling item here.**

The most interesting thing about your company is the data you hold, and **nobody outside the
company knows it exists.** The dropout's demo gets retweeted; the quant kid has a leaderboard. You
have a private repo and a demo video with no URL.

A short public artifact — *"What 25 recruiters told us is actually broken"*, or *"What 200,000 San
Francisco engineer profiles say about who's really in the market"* — does four things at once:
proves the thesis in public, creates the first inbound you've ever had, becomes a link in every
form, and **is itself the distribution play you claim to be building.** A weekend of work, and it
is the only item on this page with genuine upside rather than defence.

**5. 🟠 Reverse-diligence one portfolio company per program.**

You are the only applicants alive who can send a16z or Greylock a real memo on the hiring gaps at
three of their own portfolio companies — with named candidates. That is not an essay move, it's a
cold open, and it demonstrates the product instead of describing it. See `networking.md`.

**6. 🟠 One customer sentence, in their words.**

There is not a single customer quote in the entire pack. Baseten's head of talent or OneRobot's
founder — *"they turned this around in two days and I asked for more"* — outweighs any paragraph
you write about yourselves. Ask one of them today.

**7. 🟠 The referrers. Still `TODO`, still one Slack message.**

Unchanged from a week ago and still worth more than any answer in this pack. Three WestBridge
partners, one message.

## What to stop doing

- **Stop adding accomplishments.** You are over the bar on capability and every additional line
  dilutes the two facts that actually differentiate you.
- **Don't compete on youth, grind, or lines of code.** You're 23 and 24 at prestigious firms.
  "Young prodigy" is not your lane and "we work 100-hour weeks around our day jobs" reads as
  *confirmation* of the side-project frame, not a rebuttal. 259 commits in five days already says
  it, once, factually.
- **Don't lead the demo with the 200K index.** Every applicant claims a scraper. Lead with the
  in-market signals and the five-minute intent loop.
- **Don't hide WestBridge.** It's the asset. But you have to **convert** it — into the judgment
  record, into the referrers, into the reverse-diligence memo, and into a resignation date.
  Unconverted, it is just the reason to say no.
